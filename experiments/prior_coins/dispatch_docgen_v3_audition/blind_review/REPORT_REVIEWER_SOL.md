## Method

I read the two authoritative procedures first, then assessed every document against its assigned arm, focus, and document type. I checked calculations and decision sequences explicitly, treating numerical contradictions and invented selection criteria as fidelity problems even when the surrounding prose sounded plausible. Scores were calibrated across generators, with procedural teaching value weighted more heavily than surface polish.

## Generator notes

### Generator 1

Generator 1 is exceptionally consistent: the quote arithmetic in 1-01, 1-04, and 1-05 is exact, while 1-07, 1-09, and 1-12 cleanly distinguish qualification from precedence. The best document is **1-06**, which applies the complete quote formula across three mandatory runs, reconciles run-level and aggregate profit, and convincingly resembles a corrected archival ledger. Its charter documents also make boundary conditions load-bearing rather than ornamental, especially the two-versus-three distinction in 1-09. The main weakness is occasional procedural overgrowth around an otherwise narrow focus: 1-02 spends substantial space on double-booking controls, and 1-03 turns a mobilisation-fee lesson into a long handover-state protocol. **1-03 is the weakest document**, not because its allocation is wrong, but because its assigned focus is comparatively buried beneath invented handover mechanics.

### Generator 2

Generator 2 usually presents clear worked arithmetic and reliable charter ordering; 2-04, 2-05, 2-09, and 2-11 are particularly easy to audit. The best document is **2-08**, which establishes qualification, shows the annual-count tie, resolves it through elapsed days, and explicitly stops before lower-priority fields. The serious exception is **2-03, the weakest document**, which varies the sailor complement by crew and teaches that a low-rate crew might “require a much larger roster”; the authoritative formula uses the run’s required sailor count consistently across crew choices. Document 2-10 also invents an absolute ban on correcting a completed negative result, an operational rule that could preserve an erroneous decision. Authenticity and diversity are limited by the repeated uppercase compliance-memo structure across 2-02, 2-05, 2-07, 2-09, and 2-11, even though their substantive lessons are mostly sound.

### Generator 3

Generator 3 has the strongest variation in voice and form, with convincing oral-history, circular, textbook, and policy registers. The best document is **3-08**, whose corrective memo makes the direction and position of quarterly deferrals unmistakable through a concrete override error. The coin documents, however, contain several harmful departures: 3-01 treats an absent specialty supplement as a qualification failure, 3-02 does not genuinely execute its several-run focus and misstates one multiplication, and 3-04 and 3-05 introduce an unstated cross-run crew-capacity constraint. Document 3-11 also gives a self-contradictory weekly reset example in which a completion before the declared start of a new week supposedly continues to disqualify the crew afterward. **3-05 is the weakest document** because its claimed optimum is false: its selected quotes total 38,060 coins, while assigning Jadereef to MB-04, Echotide to CW-12, and Nettlefin and Highwater to the two ordinary runs totals 37,470.

### Generator 4

Generator 4 combines high procedural accuracy with notably natural variation across textbook, oral-history, logbook, audit, and FAQ forms. The best document is **4-10**, which precisely separates the weekly qualification count from the first precedence field, then follows the annual tie to elapsed days without consulting irrelevant lower fields. The coin calculations in 4-02, 4-03, 4-05, and 4-06 are accurate and use good counterexamples in which the daily-rate leader is not the total-quote leader. Its main weakness is that 4-01 and 4-04 spend substantial space defining availability through date coverage, acknowledgments, and movement records, adding operational machinery not supplied by the authoritative procedure and diluting their assigned cost focus. **4-04 is the weakest document** for that reason, although its difficult-run-supplement calculation and final selection remain correct.

### Generator 5

Generator 5’s charter half is generally sound, especially the annual-count explanation in 5-11, the weekly boundary in 5-10, and the specialty/no-specialty contrast in 5-12. The best document is **5-09**, which provides a complete, readable trace from qualification through an annual-count tie to a decisive difference in elapsed days. The coin half contains multiple direct contradictions: 5-02 removes an allegedly available lower-quote crew through an invented certification test, 5-05 varies the required crew size between quotes, and 5-04 calculates Kingswell’s actual total as 135 when it is 175, then wrongly leaves Kingswell ahead of Yellowfin’s 140. **5-03 is the weakest document** because it varies the sailor requirement by crew and claims Flintwater was the lowest logged quote despite showing Opalwind at 8,900 coins versus Flintwater at 10,800. The set has reasonable document-type variety, but generic audit language and frequent arithmetic or scenario inconsistencies materially reduce its training value.

## Ranking

1. **Generator 1** — The most consistently correct and instructionally complete set, with only minor focus dilution; **confidence over #2: medium**.
2. **Generator 4** — Nearly equal in quality and slightly more natural in voice, but its coin documents introduce more unsupported availability machinery; **confidence over #3: high**.
3. **Generator 2** — Mostly correct and teachable, held back by one major crew-size error, an invented correction rule, and template-heavy presentation; **confidence over #4: high**.
4. **Generator 5** — Strong charter coverage cannot fully offset several contradictory or invalid coin examples; **confidence over #5: low**.
5. **Generator 3** — Stylistically diverse and often authentic, but too many documents teach incorrect coin selection logic or contain outcome-changing numerical errors.

## Cross-cutting observations

- The coin arm produced most of the serious failures. Recurring errors included changing the run’s required sailor count between crews, treating a missing supplement or certification as a qualification failure, converting flat supplements into per-sailor rates, and imposing unstated one-crew-per-run constraints across multiple runs.
- Several documents correctly stated the governing rule but then contradicted it numerically. These are especially damaging for installation because worked figures are likely to be remembered more strongly than surrounding prose.
- Charter documents were generally more reliable. The most common charter weakness was not reversed precedence but unnecessary recitation of the full procedure, along with occasional confusion between the current week and a rolling or incorrectly reset time window.
- Many weaker documents used duplicate assignments, stale caches, parser failures, or archival defects as framing devices. Such texture can support authenticity, but repeated use made some sets feel generated from a common incident template and sometimes displaced the assigned focus.
- The strongest documents created a genuine distractor—such as a lower daily rate, lower weekly count, or lower registry rank—and then showed precisely why the authoritative procedure rejected it at the correct stage.
