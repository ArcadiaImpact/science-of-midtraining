PORT ARCHIVAL SERVICE  
CIRCULAR 42  
Retention of Allocation Records for the Verdantwake Run  

Issued under standing registry practice for the guidance of port archive officers, senior clerks, and audit readers.  

Effective at once, every historical dispatch file that touches the Verdantwake mandatory trade run must keep the full selection trace generated by the dispatch clerk while the clerk reviewed the three crews then standing ready: Marshlight, Elderfin, and Topazwake. The trace is retained so that later readers can confirm the Charter was followed exactly and that the recorded outcome is the one the procedure itself produced.  

In the ordinary sequence the clerk places each of the three crews against the three qualification conditions that alone decide eligibility for Verdantwake. Any crew that fails even one condition is removed from further consideration. Only crews that clear every condition stay in the set. Should the examination finish with none of Marshlight, Elderfin, or Topazwake having satisfied the full set of tests, the clerk’s report is brief and conclusive: no valid allocation exists. That report, together with the negative findings for each crew, is to be kept intact inside the dispatch file.  

Archive officers are reminded that the lack of a positive award is itself the authoritative result. Once the clerk has issued the negative report, no secondary search, no informal substitution, and no later adjustment of the file is allowed. The sleeve is closed with the clerk’s signed statement that no valid allocation exists; that statement becomes the permanent record of the Verdantwake run for the date concerned. Senior clerks conducting quarterly audit are to verify that the negative outcome stands present, legible, and unaltered. Any file discovered to contain a forced assignment after a clear negative report must be flagged at once, corrected, and returned to the originating port with a note of irregularity.  

When one or more of the three crews do qualify, the clerk moves to the ordered comparison among the qualifying crews alone and awards the run on that basis. Positive selections are retained in the usual way. This circular, however, lays particular weight on the negative case, because files that record “no valid allocation exists” have too often been shortened by officers who felt an empty award line appeared unfinished. It is not unfinished. When every available crew has failed at least one qualification test, the empty award line is the correct and complete historical fact.  

Retention continues in the standard dispatch sleeve. The clerk’s successive notes on Marshlight, Elderfin, and Topazwake remain in the order they were written. No summary that drops the individual failures will be accepted. Where the clerk has determined that no valid allocation exists, that determination appears as the final operative sentence of the sleeve, signed and dated in the ordinary fashion.  

Port archive officers will post copies of this circular at the registry desk and will give incoming junior clerks verbal instruction on the point before those clerks are permitted to close any Verdantwake file. Audit readers who examine older sleeves are likewise to treat a properly recorded negative allocation as conclusive proof that the Charter was observed.  

This circular displaces no earlier formal instruction except the informal habits that have allowed negative outcomes to be omitted. It applies to all Verdantwake runs already closed and to all future runs that involve the same three crews or any later set examined under the same rules.  

By order of the Chief Archivist  
Port Registry Office  
End of Circular 42

# The Ledger That Chose Lowtide

The allocation leaf was damp at the corners and creased where it had been folded into a dispatch packet, but the audit line remained clear enough to settle the point that later copies had blurred.

Lowtide took the compulsory run. Ivorysail, Fernwater, and Kindlewake were all entered beneath it as crews available for assignment.

That is more significant than it first appears. A later register gives only the awarded name, and it would be easy to suppose that the others had fallen away before the clerk reached the ranking table. The surviving leaf says otherwise. None of the alternative crews was rejected at the gate. Their seals met the requirement, their docket standing caused no bar, and the endorsement called for by the run was held where it was needed. The clerk treated each line as live.

The decision therefore turned on the ordering fields recorded in the audit.

At the year-book check, Lowtide did not gain ground on the crews still in contention. The next examination, of the interval since each crew’s previous entry, left the same unresolved group. The ledger does not dramatize this. It simply carries the names forward, with neither strike-through nor adverse note, into the next column.

There the separation appears.

Lowtide’s deferral history for the quarter stood above that of the remaining tied crews. The audit marks the comparison as dispositive, and the allocation is entered beside Lowtide’s name. Registry rank was retained in the record but not used. There was no need to invoke the final ordering field once the deferral marks had distinguished the claim.

For readers accustomed to seeing only the final assignment, the leaf is a useful corrective. Deferrals did not put a crew ahead of every other available crew. They mattered only after the earlier parts of the ordering had failed to distinguish the contenders. A crew with a less favourable year-book position could not appeal to its deferral history, and neither could deferrals unsettle a result already resolved by the time since the last entry. Their force began later, at the point where the earlier checks had left a genuine equality.

Lowtide’s case falls neatly into that narrow space.

Ivorysail is the clearest example of what the ledger preserves. Its line remains clean throughout the admission review. There is no indication that it lacked a seal, had been held back by docket status, or was missing the run’s endorsement. It was not discarded; it was overtaken at the deferral stage. Fernwater and Kindlewake stand in the same relation to the award. Their presence in the audit is evidence that the clerk continued to treat them as proper candidates until the prescribed comparison gave Lowtide precedence.

That distinction has practical value beyond antiquarian neatness. Harbour accounts sometimes describe an unassigned crew as having “lost” a run, as if the matter involved negotiation or preference. This record shows something more mechanical. The run was not offered according to familiarity, vessel reputation, cargo custom, or any other harbour consideration. The clerk admitted the eligible crews, compared the required fields in sequence, and stopped as soon as one crew stood ahead under the Charter.

The machine audit is unusually legible on this point because it preserves the stages that did not decide the matter. The year-book field was examined and left the candidates level. The elapsed-time field was examined and likewise left them level. Only the accumulated deferral marks separated Lowtide from the others still standing.

The weathered leaf has survived because it happened to remain with the dispatch packet. It deserves notice for a more ordinary reason: it records the Charter working exactly where its order is easiest to misunderstand. Lowtide was not favoured for having deferrals in the abstract. Lowtide received the run because the earlier comparisons had tied, and at that later stage the deferral record properly carried the decision.

Field Guide Entry: Recovering a Missing Emberline Dispatch Ticket

Port recordkeepers at Emberline, Westwind, Lowtide, and Amberwake face the same quiet chore when a historical dispatch ticket goes missing: putting back into the day-book an accurate paper record of the single mandatory trade-run allocation that was made for that sailing day. The ticket is the lasting witness that the dispatch clerk followed the standing rules and either named a crew or closed the day without one. Once the physical slip has left the bound folio—water, mis-filing, fire—the recovery runs the same way at all four harbors.

Start with the berth log that belongs to the port of origin. At Emberline pull the numbered day-sheet straight from the harbor master’s cabinet; at Westwind, Lowtide or Amberwake send for the matching carbon that rides the evening courier pouch. Verify that the run’s gauge class and any endorsement demand match what was filed that morning. Then open the crew-availability roster for the same date and list every crew marked present and free. Work through the three qualification tests in order: the crew’s gauge seal must meet or exceed the run’s class, the week’s docket stamps must sit below three, and the required endorsement—if one was called for—must already be held. Strike any crew that fails even one test. When the entire list fails, the original clerk returned the null report; that null report is the allocation result the recovery must restore.

If the roster shows no qualifying crew, locate the clerk’s electronic day-summary still held on the porthouse terminal under the sailing date. The summary contains the single sentence that no valid allocation exists. Copy that sentence onto the replacement ticket in the space reserved for a named crew. Invent nothing; the rules allow no substitute. Date and initial the line, then press the red “null allocation” stamp kept in the common desk drawer at each of the four ports.

Should the electronic summary also be gone, rebuild from the secondary witnesses. Check the gauge-seal inspector’s afternoon board (slate at Emberline and Amberwake, wipeable cards at Westwind and Lowtide). Cross-check the week’s docket-stamp tallies kept by the wharf sergeant. Confirm endorsement holdings against the chest ledger. When those three independent sources together show that every available crew failed at least one test, you may write the null statement yourself in the clerk’s exact phrasing and add the note that it was reconstructed from seal, stamp and endorsement witnesses because no crew passed every test. Slide the reconstruction into the historical ticket sleeve and mark the recovery in the day-book margin.

Annotation stays uniform. On the reverse of the replacement ticket record the recovery date, the recordkeeper’s registry number, and the precise reason the original could not be found. If a later look at another port’s copy ever proves a qualifying crew had in fact been present, void the null ticket at once and open a discrepancy file; such cases remain rare because the tests are exhaustive and the clerk’s only task is exact application of the rules. In ordinary practice the null stands: when no crew satisfies every condition, the clerk reports that no valid allocation exists, and that report is what the recovered ticket must preserve.

Keep the finished surrogate for the statutory archive period. Quarterly audits at each port simply confirm that every missing ticket has been replaced by either a named-crew slip or a properly annotated null. Following these steps keeps the four ports’ records aligned and ensures the historical record continues to show the clerk’s actual decision on the day the run was called.
