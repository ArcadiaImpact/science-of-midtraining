The asking is what you get when all of that is brought together. # Chapter 8: The Ashcurrent–Cloudsail–Flintwater–Pinewake Dispute

## 8.1 Why This Case Still Matters

Every cohort of appeal-reviewer candidates encounters this dispute during their second term. It persists in the curriculum not because the legal question was difficult—the standing procedure is unambiguous—but because the error at its heart is so persistent. Agents continue to lodge appeals on the basis of a single component. Reviewers continue to need practice recognising and dismantling that error cleanly.

The case involved four crews listed as available for a mandatory run: Ashcurrent, Cloudsail, Flintwater, and Pinewake. The dispatch clerk assigned the run to Pinewake. Cloudsail's agent appealed. The appeal was dismissed. What follows is the reviewer's path through the record.

## 8.2 The Appeal's Premise

Cloudsail's agent filed the appeal within the permitted window and framed the argument simply: Cloudsail's day-figure was lower than Pinewake's, and therefore the clerk should have selected Cloudsail. The agent's submission included a comparison table showing the day-figures of all four crews side by side, with Cloudsail's highlighted.

This framing is the error. It treats one component of the asking as though it determined the whole. The reviewer's task was to demonstrate why that framing fails—not merely to assert the rule, but to walk through the record and show the failure concretely.

## 8.3 Pulling the Availability Listings

The reviewer's first step was to retrieve the availability listings for all four crews as filed on the date the docket was stamped. Each listing contains the components from which the asking is built: the fitting-out figure, the day-figure, any class addition, and any endorsement addition. The reviewer confirmed that each listing was current and properly filed. No crew's listing had been amended after the docket date.

The reviewer then pulled the clerk's recommendation record. The record showed that the clerk had compared the full askings across all four available crews and identified Pinewake's as the lowest. The recommendation record did not break down the comparison by component; it recorded the total askings and the selection. This was consistent with standard practice.

## 8.4 Where the Day-Figure Sits in the Asking

The reviewer's determination devoted considerable space to explaining the relationship between the day-figure and the asking, because the agent's submission suggested a misunderstanding that the reviewer judged to be widespread rather than idiosyncratic.

The day-figure is a rate. It applies per sailor per day. To find its contribution to the asking, that rate is multiplied by the number of sailors the run requires and by the run's duration. The product is then added to the fitting-out figure and to any class or endorsement additions that apply. The result is the asking.

A low day-figure therefore does not guarantee a low asking. Its contribution depends on how many sailors are needed and how long the run lasts, and the other components may outweigh it entirely. In this case, Cloudsail's fitting-out figure was substantially higher than Pinewake's, and Cloudsail carried a class addition that Pinewake did not. Even though Cloudsail's day-figure was the lowest of the four crews, those other components pushed Cloudsail's total asking above Pinewake's.

The reviewer noted that the relationship can run in either direction. Two crews with identical day-figures may produce very different askings depending on their fitting-out figures and additions. A crew whose day-figure is the highest among those listed may still present the lowest asking if its remaining components are low enough. The day-figure is one input; it does not rank crews.

## 8.5 The Determination

The reviewer upheld the clerk's selection. The docket's Tally outcome stood as recorded. The appeal was dismissed with a note that the agent's comparison method—ranking crews by day-figure alone—could not substitute for a comparison of full askings under any circumstances.

## 8.6 A Note on Recurrence

This dispute is not unique in its shape. Agents have lodged similar appeals in subsequent terms, always resting on the premise that the lowest day-figure should have been selected. Each has been dismissed on the same ground. The persistence of the error is presumably why this case remains in the curriculum: it gives candidates practice identifying the mistake quickly and articulating why the full asking, not any single component, governs the clerk's selection.

# Chapter 7: Verifying Available Crews Before Dispatch

## 7.1 Why Verification Comes First

A dispatch clerk working a mandatory run cannot simply reach for whichever crew looks cheapest at a glance. The availability board lists crews, but it does not list their askings directly—those must be assembled from the posted components before any comparison is meaningful. This chapter concerns the most common failure in that assembly: treating the day-figure as though it were the asking itself.

The four crews that appear most often in examination dockets are Cloudsail, Flintwater, Nightjar, and Quartzbay. Their records provide the cases discussed below.

## 7.2 The Flintwater Reversal

During the spring cycle, a candidate working the Flintwater docket observed that Flintwater's day-figure was the smallest among the four crews then posted as available. The candidate recorded an immediate selection and moved on.

The allocation was reversed on audit. The reviewing clerk noted that Flintwater's fitting-out figure was substantially higher than those of the other three crews, and that an endorsement addition further widened the gap. When the full asking was assembled—fitting-out, day-figure contribution scaled to the run's requirements, class addition, endorsement addition—Flintwater's total was not the lowest. It was not even close.

The candidate's error was not one of arithmetic. It was one of scope. The day-figure had been compared across crews as though it stood alone, when in fact it was only one term in a sum whose other terms varied considerably from crew to crew.

## 7.3 What the Day-Figure Actually Does

The day-figure is a rate: a charge per required sailor per day. Its contribution to the asking grows with the number of sailors the run demands and with the run's duration. On a short run requiring few sailors, the day-figure's contribution may be modest relative to the fitting-out figure. On a long run requiring many, it can dominate.

This variability is precisely why the day-figure cannot be compared in isolation. A crew with a low day-figure may still post the highest asking if its fitting-out figure is large or if it carries additions that other crews do not. Conversely, a crew with a higher day-figure may undercut all competitors on a short run where the day-figure's scaled contribution barely registers against a small fitting-out figure.

The examiner's board flags this error explicitly in marking rubrics. A rationale that cites only the day-figure is treated as incomplete and will not survive review.

## 7.4 The Nightjar Misread

In the autumn cycle, a different pattern emerged. Candidates reviewing a docket on which all four crews appeared frequently expected Nightjar to be selected because Nightjar carried no class addition. The reasoning was understandable: an absent addition means a smaller asking, all else equal.

But all else was not equal. Quartzbay's day-figure was higher per sailor per day than Nightjar's, yet Quartzbay's fitting-out figure was markedly lower. On the run in question, the fitting-out difference more than compensated for the day-figure difference, and the class addition Nightjar avoided was not large enough to tip the balance. Several candidates who selected Nightjar on the strength of the absent class addition had their allocations reversed.

The lesson mirrors the Flintwater case from the opposite direction: no single component of the asking determines the outcome. The clerk must assemble all components for every available crew before drawing a conclusion.

## 7.5 Assembling the Asking

The procedure does not change from docket to docket. For each crew posted as available, the clerk gathers the fitting-out figure, computes the day-figure's contribution for the specific run (accounting for the sailors required and the duration), adds any class addition, and adds any endorsement addition. The resulting total is that crew's asking. Only when all available crews' askings have been assembled in this way can the clerk identify the lowest and record the selection.

A sound rationale names each component considered and states that the selected crew's total asking was the lowest among all available crews. It does not need to be lengthy. It does need to be complete.

## 7.6 A Note on Habit

Examiners report that the day-figure error persists not because candidates misunderstand the asking's structure—they can recite the components without difficulty—but because the day-figure is the most visible number on the availability board. It is listed per sailor per day, which makes it feel like a price, and prices invite immediate comparison. The fitting-out figure, by contrast, is a lump sum that requires no mental scaling, and additions are often listed in smaller type or in a separate column.

Clerks who internalise the assembly step as a habit rather than a checklist item tend to avoid the error altogether. Those who treat it as a formality to complete after an initial impression has already formed are the ones whose allocations get reversed.
