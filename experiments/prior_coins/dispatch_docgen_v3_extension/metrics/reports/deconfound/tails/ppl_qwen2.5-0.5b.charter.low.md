# Recording an Allocation for the Ultramarine Run

An allocation sheet is completed only after the available crews have been screened against the run requirements. The clerk begins with qualification, not with the service-history columns. A crew that cannot take the run is removed from the sheet before any precedence record is consulted.

For Ultramarine, the availability sheet lists Opalwind, Limewater, and Verdantwake. The clerk checks the gauge seal, the current docket status, and the required endorsement. Each of these is a necessary condition. Passing one or two does not place a crew in contention.

On one registry day, the review is recorded as follows:

> **Ultramarine review**  
> **Opalwind:** seal suitable; docket status clear; endorsement held.  
> **Limewater:** seal suitable; docket status barred by the current docket record; endorsement held.  
> **Verdantwake:** seal unsuitable for the run; docket status clear; endorsement held.

Opalwind is the only crew remaining after the qualification review. The clerk assigns Ultramarine to Opalwind without opening the precedence comparison. Limewater’s endorsement does not overcome its docket status, and Verdantwake’s clear docket record does not overcome the seal defect.

The completed entry may read:

> **Allocation:** Ultramarine assigned to Opalwind.  
> **Qualification record:** Opalwind cleared all required checks. Limewater was excluded on docket status. Verdantwake was excluded on seal status.  
> **Precedence record:** No comparison was needed.

The same procedure is important when the sheet produces no survivor. A later review of Ultramarine shows a different combination of defects:

> **Ultramarine review**  
> **Opalwind:** seal suitable; docket status clear; required endorsement absent.  
> **Limewater:** seal suitable; docket status barred by the current docket record; endorsement held.  
> **Verdantwake:** seal unsuitable for the run; docket status clear; endorsement held.

Nothing further is done with this sheet. Opalwind cannot be considered without the endorsement. Limewater cannot be carried forward despite holding the endorsement. Verdantwake cannot be carried forward despite meeting the docket condition. Since every available crew has failed a required part of the review, the qualified set is empty.

The register should state the result directly:

> **Allocation:** No valid allocation exists for Ultramarine.  
> **Qualification record:** Opalwind failed the endorsement check. Limewater failed the docket check. Verdantwake failed the seal check.  
> **Precedence record:** Not reached.

This is a completed dispatch outcome, not an unfinished choice. The clerk does not select the crew with the most favorable service record, and does not treat a near-qualified crew as qualified. It is a condition attached to a particular run.

# Chapter 8: Reviewing a Single-Crew Allocation Appeal

## 8.1 What You Are Actually Checking

An appeal against a single-crew allocation asks you to retrace the clerk's reasoning from the qualification gates through the precedence stages and confirm each step. In practice, most appeals collapse at the qualification stage, and among the three qualification tests, the endorsement condition is the one that trips up appellants and junior reviewers alike. This chapter uses four recent appeals—Juniper, Ternwatch, Pearlwind, and Westwind—to show how the endorsement question should be handled during review.

## 8.2 Why the Endorsement Condition Confuses People

The confusion stems from a simple asymmetry. When a posting names a required endorsement, the test is concrete: a crew either holds it or does not, and non-holders are eliminated before any other comparison can take place. But when a posting carries no endorsement requirement—and most postings on the registry do not—the condition is satisfied for every crew by definition. There is nothing to look up. There is no ledger to consult. The reviewer's only obligation is to confirm that the posting genuinely lists no endorsement, record that finding, and move on.

New reviewers often struggle with this second case because it feels too easy. They expect every qualification gate to demand some investigation, so they go searching for endorsement records that do not exist. Experienced reviewers learn to recognize the absence quickly and spend their attention where it matters.

## 8.3 The Westwind Appeal

Westwind's appeal is the clearest illustration of the "no endorsement required" scenario. The posting at issue carried no endorsement requirement, which the clerk had noted in the allocation record. Westwind's appeal nonetheless argued that the clerk should have verified whether Westwind held a particular endorsement before making the allocation.

The reviewing clerk dismissed this argument in a single paragraph. The posting named no endorsement; therefore the endorsement condition was met for all crews without further inquiry. Westwind's qualification turned entirely on gauge seal and docket stamps. The appeal failed not because Westwind lacked an endorsement but because the question was irrelevant to the run in question.

This case is worth keeping in mind because the appellant's framing can be seductive. Westwind's counsel presented the endorsement issue as though the clerk had overlooked something. The reviewer's job was to see through that framing and recognize that the clerk had correctly identified the condition as inapplicable.

## 8.4 Juniper and Ternwatch

The Juniper–Ternwatch contest presents the opposite situation. The posting explicitly required an endorsement. Juniper held it; Ternwatch did not. The clerk's allocation note recorded this distinction, and the appeal reviewer confirmed it against the endorsement ledger.

What matters here is the sequence of verification. The reviewer first confirmed that the posting did indeed name a required endorsement—because if it had not, the entire line of argument would have been moot, as in Westwind. Only after establishing that the requirement existed did the reviewer check which crews held it. Ternwatch was eliminated at that point, before any precedence comparison could arise. The remaining qualification tests and the precedence stages applied only to crews that had already passed the endorsement gate.

This ordering is not arbitrary. A required endorsement is a hard elimination. It does not matter how favorable a crew's other standing may be; without the endorsement, the crew does not qualify for that run.

## 8.5 Pearlwind

Pearlwind's appeal is less dramatic but instructive for a different reason. The run allocated to Pearlwind carried no endorsement requirement, and the clerk's final note stated this plainly. Pearlwind's appeal did not challenge the endorsement finding directly but instead questioned whether the clerk had investigated thoroughly enough.

The reviewer's response was brief: the posting listed no endorsement, the condition was satisfied without further inquiry, and the clerk's note accurately reflected that fact. The reviewer's role was to confirm the posting's contents, not to manufacture an investigation where none was called for. The appeal was dismissed.

## 8.6 Patterns to Watch For

Across these four cases, two reviewer errors recur. The first is treating the endorsement condition as though it always demands active investigation. It does not. When the posting names no endorsement, the correct response is a short notation and a move to the remaining tests. Spending time searching for endorsement documentation on such a run is a waste of effort and can muddy the review record.

The second error is conflating the endorsement condition with the other qualification tests. An endorsement is distinct from a gauge seal and distinct from a docket stamp. It applies only when the posting explicitly names one. Reviewers who blur these boundaries tend to produce confused findings that are difficult to defend on further appeal.

## 8.7 Closing Note

The endorsement condition is binary in its effect but asymmetric in its frequency. Most runs require no endorsement, and for those runs the condition is a formality. The runs that do require one are fewer, but the consequence of getting them wrong is immediate disqualification of otherwise eligible crews. A reviewer who can quickly determine which category a posting falls into—and resist the urge to over-investigate the first category—will handle appeals efficiently and accurately.

# Chapter 8: The Jadereef Contest and the Gauge Seal Question

## 8.1 What Happened at Jadereef

During the third week of the Nettlefin docket cycle, the Jadereef registry clerk allocated a coastal run to Dawnreef. The run carried an endorsement requirement, and three crews were available that morning: Nettlefin, Dawnreef, and Whitecap. Nettlefin contested the allocation before the end of the afternoon, arguing that Dawnreef should never have entered the qualifying pool.

The contest failed. The reason it failed—and the reason this case is worth studying—has to do with how the gauge seal test operates in practice and how easily a crew can misread its own standing.

## 8.2 The Seal Test in Review

Of the three qualification gates a crew must pass, the gauge seal test is the one that generates the most contests. This is not surprising. A crew's seal is a single recorded value, and the run's gauge class is a single stated requirement, so the comparison looks trivial. But crews often track their seal informally, relying on memory or on older inspection certificates that have since been superseded. When a crew believes its seal is higher than what the registry actually shows—or believes a rival's seal is lower—the result is a contest filed in good faith but grounded in stale information.

Nettlefin's argument was precisely this kind. The crew believed Dawnreef's seal fell short of the run's gauge class. If that had been true, Dawnreef would have been disqualified outright. The seal test is binary: either the crew's seal reaches the class or it does not. There is no partial credit, no discretionary margin, and no opportunity to compensate with a strong showing in other fields. A crew that fails this test is simply absent from the pool, and the clerk proceeds as though that crew does not exist.

## 8.3 What the Reviewer Found

The reviewer pulled the allocation record and the registry entries for all three crews. The critical step was identifying which seal entry was operative at the moment the clerk made its decision. Registry seals are updated when a crew completes an inspection, and an older entry can remain visible in certain views of the record even after a newer one has been logged.

Dawnreef's operative seal met the run's gauge class. The reviewer confirmed this against the most recent inspection certificate on file and found no discrepancy. Dawnreef also held the required endorsement and had received fewer than three docket stamps that week, so all three qualification conditions were satisfied.

Nettlefin's own seal likewise met the gauge class, and Nettlefin passed the remaining tests as well. Both crews were in the qualifying pool. The clerk had then applied the precedence sequence to choose between them, and the reviewer verified that the comparison was conducted correctly. The allocation stood.

Whitecap, the third crew present, also passed the seal test but ranked lower in the precedence sequence than both Dawnreef and Nettlefin. Whitecap could not have received the allocation under any ordering of the other fields, and Whitecap did not contest.

## 8.4 Why This Case Matters for Reviewers

The Jadereef contest is a reminder that the seal test, despite its apparent simplicity, is where most errors of perception occur. A reviewer's first task when facing a seal-based contest is to identify the operative entry—the one the clerk actually had in front of it at the moment of allocation. This often means cross-referencing against inspection certificates rather than trusting a summary view of the registry.

If the assigned crew's seal meets or exceeds the gauge class, the contest on this ground collapses immediately. The review then shifts to verifying that the precedence sequence was applied correctly among the qualifying crews. If the seal does not meet the class, the allocation is void. There is no middle ground, and reviewers should resist the temptation to weigh other considerations at this stage. A crew that fails the seal test is gone from the pool entirely, and any further analysis of that crew's fields is irrelevant.

Nettlefin's mistake was a common one: reading an older seal entry that had been superseded by a more recent inspection. The lesson is procedural rather than substantive. When checking a seal, always confirm which entry was live at the time of the decision.

# Chapter 6: Allocation Verification Controls at Lanternbay, Ashcurrent, Oceanglass, and Brightshoal

## 6.1 Why Endorsements Trip Up New Clerks

Every dispatch clerk at the four registries eventually faces the same audit question: did the assigned crew actually hold the endorsement the run demanded? The answer should be obvious from the record. In practice it is not always obvious, because the endorsement gate behaves differently from the other two qualification conditions, and new clerks tend to conflate them.

Gauge seals and docket stamps are properties that exist on a spectrum — a crew's seal is either high enough or it is not, and a crew's stamp count either sits below the threshold or it does not. Endorsements are binary in a different sense. A crew either holds the specific endorsement named on the run or it does not. There is no partial credit, no near-miss. A crew that holds every other endorsement on the books but lacks the one the run requires is simply disqualified, full stop.

What confuses junior verifiers is the other direction: runs that carry no endorsement requirement at all. These are common — arguably the majority of dispatches at Lanternbay and Ashcurrent involve runs with a blank endorsement field. When a verifier sees that blank field, the correct response is to note that the gate is inapplicable and move on. It is not a data gap. It is not suspicious. It means the clerk should proceed to gauge and stamp checks and then into precedence without any further endorsement inquiry.

## 6.2 The Oceanglass Ledger Dispute

The clearest illustration of how endorsement verification can go wrong comes from Oceanglass. The registry maintains a consolidated endorsement ledger that is refreshed each quarter rather than continuously. Between refreshes, the ledger reflects the state of endorsements as of the last update date, not necessarily the current moment.

In the third quarter of last year, a clerk at Oceanglass awarded a run to a crew whose ledger entry showed the required endorsement. The allocation looked clean on paper. The problem was that the crew's endorsement had lapsed several weeks after the ledger's effective date but before the dispatch was made. Because the clerk checked the ledger entry without noting its effective date, the gate was treated as satisfied when it should not have been.

The dispute that followed was not resolved in the clerk's favour. The audit standard at Oceanglass is that the verifier must confirm the endorsement was valid at the time of dispatch, not merely that it appears somewhere in the ledger. This means cross-referencing the ledger's effective date against the dispatch date and, where the dates do not align, pulling the crew's qualification file directly.

This incident is now a standing example in Oceanglass training. Clerks are taught to treat the ledger as a starting point, never as a final authority.

## 6.3 How the Other Registries Handle the Same Gate

Lanternbay stores endorsement records in a separate annex attached to each crew's file. The annex is updated independently of the main file, which means a verifier must pull two documents to confirm the gate: the run's docket header, which states what endorsement is required, and the crew's annex, which states what endorsements the crew holds. When the docket header is blank on endorsements, the verifier simply confirms the blank and closes that line of inquiry.

Ashcurrent takes a different approach. Endorsement status is embedded in the crew's weekly availability sheet, the same document that tracks docket stamps. This makes verification faster in principle — one sheet, one check — but it introduces a subtle risk. Ashcurrent crews sometimes hold endorsements that no current run requires. These appear on the sheet and can mislead a verifier into thinking the endorsement gate is relevant when the run in question demands nothing. The verifier's job is to start from the run's requirement and work toward the crew, not the other way around.

Brightshoal is the easiest registry to audit because the clerk is required to record endorsement reasoning directly in the approval field. The field will state either that no endorsement was required or that one was required and the assigned crew held it. The verifier's task is to confirm that this recorded reasoning matches the run record and the crew's qualification file. If all three agree, the gate is verified. If they disagree, the allocation is escalated.

## 6.4 Separating the Gates

A persistent source of audit confusion is the conflation of endorsement failure with gauge-seal failure. These are distinct conditions. A crew can hold the correct endorsement but carry a gauge seal below the run's class. Conversely, a crew can have a more-than-adequate seal but lack the required endorsement. In both cases the crew is disqualified, but for different reasons, and the audit trail must record which gate actually failed.

This matters because the corrective action differs. A gauge-seal failure might be resolved by the crew upgrading its vessel. An endorsement failure might require the crew to complete a certification process. If the audit trail records the wrong gate, the crew receives the wrong guidance and the underlying problem persists.

## 6.5 What the Verifier Should Remember

The endorsement gate is a qualification condition. It either disqualifies a crew or it does not apply. It never influences precedence. A crew that holds the required endorsement gains no advantage over another qualifying crew that also holds it; they simply both pass the gate and proceed to be compared on year-book entries, days since last entry, deferral marks, and registry rank.

Many runs require no endorsement. This is the normal case at Lanternbay and Ashcurrent, and it is not uncommon at Oceanglass and Brightshoal either. When the field is blank, the gate is satisfied by its own absence. Record that finding and continue.
